1K Chess — Queen's Pawn

David Horne, 1982 — the most famous 1K program ever written. A working chess opponent squeezed into 672 bytes of Z80, small enough to run on an unexpanded ZX81, published as a type-in listing in Your Computer. It cuts a few corners to fit and won't trouble a club player, but that it plays chess at all in a kilobyte is the achievement everyone remembers.

You always play Black; the computer plays White and moves first. This is the Queen's Pawn edition — the computer opens by nudging its queen's pawn forward. (The original was sold with two versions on the two sides of one cassette; the other opens with the king's pawn, and sits one menu entry away.)

Getting started

  1. The computer makes the first move — it advances a pawn a single square (see the note below).
  2. Enter your reply as two board squares (details under Controls).
  3. The computer thinks, then replies; play continues until someone is mated.

Watch it think. With almost no memory to hide in, the program does its searching in plain sight — as the computer decides, you can see it work through candidate moves right on the board. It's a rare, honest look at a chess engine turning over its options, and it's normal; just let it settle on a move.

Controls — moves are entered "backwards"

1K Chess uses backward algebraic notation: each square is typed rank first, then file — the reverse of the usual "e" then "7". Give the square you're moving from, then the square you're moving to, four characters in all. So a knight from g8 to f6 is 8G6F, and a pawn from e7 to e5 is 7E5E. It feels alien for a move or two, then your fingers adjust.

A gamepad has no role here — moves are typed as coordinates, so 1K Chess is keyboard by design.

Rules it leaves out

To fit in a kilobyte, three rules were dropped. The program simply doesn't know them, so don't try:

  • No castling. Your king and rooks never castle — bring the rooks out by hand instead.
  • No en passant.
  • No pawn promotion. A pawn that reaches the far rank stays a pawn.

Playing it

  • Take the free tempo. The computer's opening pawn only advances one square rather than the usual two, which already hands you a small edge. Develop normally and use it.
  • Expect eccentric play. There's no opening book and only shallow look-ahead, so the computer's moves can be strange — it may not see a capture staring it in the face. Play solid chess and you'll usually come out ahead.
  • No undo and no hints. There isn't room in a kilobyte for either — think before you commit a move.

Play it in one sitting — 1K Chess is a single-session game. There's no save state, and no way to store a position: with only a kilobyte to work in, the program has nowhere to keep one, and this machine can't snapshot it for you. Your game lives only as long as this tab stays open — close it or reload and the board goes back to the start. Settle in and finish before you wander off.
