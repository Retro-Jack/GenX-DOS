IBM · Personal Computer

IBM PC — Controls

The IBM Personal Computer (1981) and its DOS, running in your browser. An 8088 drawing four colours at 320×200 on a CGA card, or sixteen on an EGA one, with a single-bit speaker for the music — the machine that took the office and then, slowly and against everyone's expectations, the games. Click the screen to enable sound.

Controls — your keyboard is the PC's keyboard

There is no translation layer here worth the name: a key you press arrives as the same key on the PC. Letters, digits, punctuation, the function keys, the numeric keypad and the arrows all pass straight through, which is what these games expect. What follows is only the handful worth saying out loud.

KeyWhat it does
Ctrl and AltReach the game, not the browser. Several titles here use them as ordinary controls — they are Commander Keen's jump and pogo stick.
F1–F12Reach the game. Digger's sound and music are on F7 and F9, Round 42 fires on F1.
Numeric keypadPasses through as the keypad, arrows and all. Several games want it specifically — Paratrooper and Round 42 both steer with it.
Tab, arrows, SpaceGo to the game rather than scrolling the page.
Ctrl+C / Ctrl+VLeft to the browser, so copy and paste still work.

This machine is keyboard-only — there is no mouse and no gamepad. Nothing here needs either: every game on the shelf was written for a keyboard, and several of them for a keyboard that had no arrow keys separate from the keypad at all.

Starting up

The screen stays dark for a moment while the machine starts, exactly as a real one would — there is nothing to see until the program has something to draw. Then the game appears, already running. Nothing needs typing at a C:\> prompt: each title arrives with its own start-up written for it. The exception is MS-DOS 4.00, which is the prompt: it boots, asks for the date and the time, and then waits for you at A>.

What each game asks of the machine

These games were written across nine years of a fast-moving machine, and they do not all want the same computer. Each one is given the hardware it was written for, and that is why the picture and the pace differ between them:

SettingWhich games, and why
CGA, four coloursMost of the shelf. The black, cyan, magenta and white — or black, red, green and brown — that anyone who used a PC before 1987 remembers whether they want to or not.
EGA, sixteen coloursCaptain Comic and Commander Keen, the two latest arrivals, and the reason they look a generation ahead of the rest.
VGAMS-DOS 4.00, which is not a game at all and wants nothing more exotic than a machine that boots.
A slowed processorDigger, Sopwith, Beast, Rogue and Kroz. These were written for a machine of a particular speed and have no idea that faster ones were coming; left alone they run at a pace no one could play.

Sound

Everything you hear is the PC's internal speaker: one bit, one voice, on or off, driven fast enough to fool the ear into hearing a pitch. It is a terrible instrument and the people who wrote for it did remarkable things anyway. As with every page here, sound begins after your first keypress — browsers will not make a noise until you have touched the page.

Saving

Nothing is kept between visits. Games that save — Rogue and Commander Keen both do — will write their file happily enough, and it will be gone when you close the tab. Rogue's high scores and Kroz's, likewise. Anything you create at the MS-DOS prompt goes the same way — write a batch file, run it, close the tab, and the disk is as it was. Play them as arcade machines rather than as campaigns.
