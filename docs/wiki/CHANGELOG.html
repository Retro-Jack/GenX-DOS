[Unreleased]

Added

  • An ARCADE branch: ten makers, ten games each. C:\SYSTEMS\ARCADE\<MAKER>\GAMES, the same shape as CONSOLE\NES\GAMES, sitting at row 4 of the EMULATOR LAUNCHER with the wiki moved to 5. Makers are listed alphabetically, matching the CONSOLE menu — a menu ordered by a private ranking isn't self-evident to anyone but its author. Games run year ascending within each maker, and every launcher is named after its romset as the site's other launchers are. All 100 romsets are built and verified byte-exact against the core's own metadata — every member decompressed and its CRC recomputed, with no absent, wrong or extra file in any of them. They were assembled by CRC rather than by filename, because the local libraries are a modern set and the core wants what its own data file lists: the two disagree on filenames constantly and on which archive a chip lives in occasionally. Five chips were missing from the main library entirely — two Galaga colour PROMs, a Sheriff colour PROM, Bubble Bobble's MCU ROM and a Space Fever program ROM — and turned up in the other local collections, so nothing needed sourcing. Pairing the title list against the romset list by position would have filed four games under the wrong ROM — Battlezone as missile, Marble Madness as paperboy, Sheriff as spacefev, Bionic Commando as blktiger. Both lists were year-ascending but broke ties differently. Matched on each set's own recorded title instead. 97 of the 100 carry marquee artwork, normalised to the same 674x145 canvas as the originals. Gauntlet's is the one that needed cutting: the scan is a blank marquee, the version with an empty white instruction panel below the logo, so it is clipped to the wordmark. Every other candidate turned out to be legitimate artwork whose whiteness is clouds, star fields or a white ground — a whiteness test flags nine of them, and cropping on that basis removed Space Fever's title outright. What actually distinguishes a blank panel is a large featureless white block low down on a coloured ground with the art above it, and exactly one of the 91 matches that. The three without are all Irem — Traverse USA, Image Fight, Ninja Spirit — and a cabinet with no art shows an unlit marquee, which is a thing you actually saw in an arcade, rather than a broken-image icon, which is not.

Changed

  • The five twin-stick cabinets use both sticks. Robotron: 2084 and Splat! move with one hand and fire with the other; Battlezone and Sheriff drive a tank tread with each; Inferno moves and aims. The core reads those as a separate second joystick fed from the pad's right analogue stick, so a D-pad alone could never reach them — on Battlezone the tank drove but could not steer. The right stick is now bound (and I/J/K/L on the keyboard), and the arrow keys drive the left stick as well as the D-pad, so movement is the arrow keys on all hundred machines. Verified on both: holding one tread on Battlezone pivots the tank, and the right stick on Robotron kills robots.

  • Manufacturer rows carry a date range. A maker that spans several machines — Acorn 1981-1986, Commodore 1977-1984, Atari 1977-1986 on the console side — was showing a single year, which was really just its earliest machine. Eight rows across the HOME COMPUTERS and CONSOLE menus now show the span; a maker with one machine keeps its one year. The year field widened to hold a range and is right-aligned, so all 479 dated rows on the site still close their bracket in the same column.

  • Gamedocs for the arcade section, all one hundred. systems/arcade/controls.html covers the shared panel — six buttons, the coin door, the two-stick machines, and the cabinets whose control was a trackball or a spinner — and indexes all 100 games by maker. All 100 gamedocs are written: controls on every one, a strategy section on every one, and scoring on 58. The 42 without scoring say so and why, rather than carrying invented numbers — Irem's ten have no guides with point values at all, and the early Nintendo cabinets (Radar Scope, Sky Skipper, HeliFire, Space Firebird, Space Fever, Sheriff) are too obscure to be documented anywhere on the site. GameFAQs has no arcade entry whatsoever for Gauntlet, Green Beret, Gun.Smoke or Strider. The controls in them are not recalled: the core ships its own per-game control labels, and they name the buttons for 82 of the 100. That is why Asteroids reads Rotate Left / Rotate Right rather than a stick (its metadata claims an 8-way; the game has no up or down), Missile Command's three buttons read as its three missile bases, and Tempest's second button is the Superzapper. tools/arcade-labels.py corrects that data where it shouts in capitals, spells hyperspace three ways, or names a control the machine does not have — as an explicit list, so a wrong correction is visible in one place. Scoring comes from GameFAQs, which refuses anything but a real browser session; tools/gamefaqs-lookup.js runs inside one. The guides are twenty years of different authors and have no common shape — ASCII tables in <pre>, real HTML tables that run their cells together when flattened, and prose with no scoring at all — so it tries a heading, falls back to any cluster of "N points" lines, and reports failure rather than guessing. Those guides carry explicit no-reproduction notices, so only the values are taken; every sentence on the pages is written here. Where GameFAQs had nothing, the manufacturers did. Operator's manuals carry the factory settings — lives per credit, the score a spare is awarded at — which is scoring information even when the manual never prints a point table, and arcarc.xmission.com holds 1,416 of them. They are image-only scans, so they are rendered and OCR'd; the DIP tables usually sit ten to twenty pages in, past the installation instructions. That produced eleven games' worth: Space Firebird's full point table from Nintendo, Green Beret's four-row bonus-life table from Konami, Arkanoid's and Red Alert's factory switch positions, Strider's extend table, Defender's setup booklet, and the lives-and-bonus settings for Rolling Thunder, Narc, HeliFire and Kid Niki. Chase H.Q.'s manual gave the rules instead — catching the suspect extends the timer by exactly 60 seconds, and there are three turbo bursts a round. The best source turned out to be the one furthest from the arcade: the manual for the home conversion. An arcade service manual is written for the operator — wiring, switches, parts — while the cartridge manual is written for the player and prints the point table. That is where Gauntlet's scoring came from, a game with no arcade GameFAQs entry at all, along with R-Type's thirteen-enemy bestiary, Space Harrier's ground-and-sky values and Kung-Fu Master's extra life. Each is labelled as the port's figures, because a port's manual is authoritative for the port and not necessarily for the coin-op — Arkanoid proves it, awarding a spare Vaus at 30,000/60,000/90,000 at home against the cabinet's own 20,000/60,000, so that page keeps the two apart. Flyers turned out to be the weaker source for scoring but the better one for mechanics: Irem's own sheets rewrote R-Type and Image Fight, and Moon Patrol's Japanese flyer had the checkpoint table nothing else did. Nothing from a cheats, codes or secrets section was used: Space Invaders' saucer trick and Ghosts'n Goblins' tombstone exploit were both found and both left out, and the extractor skips those sections outright.

Fixed

  • The Intellivision menu never got the column treatment. It is the one menu on the site whose echo is padded 16 spaces rather than 17, so the sweep that dropped the code column sitewide matched not one of its rows: it kept a four-column layout nothing else had, and sat a character to the left of every other box. Re-columned and re-indented; all 1,134 frame lines now share an indent, pair their corners correctly and run a uniform rule.
  • find could not see Capcom's 1942. It skipped launchers whose name is all digits, to avoid the numbered menu aliases — but those carry data and the loop already requires a link, so the test only ever hid the one game whose name really is a number.

Changed

  • The menus lost their code column. Every framed menu on the site ran four columns — number, title, year, and the DOS command that launches it — and the fourth was eating the space the titles needed. Dropping it freed eleven characters, so sixteen titles that had been squeezed to fit are now written out in full, and the year sits one space off the border instead of two. 359 dated rows and 29 yearless ones changed across 708 framed rows, every one still exactly 45 characters wide. The codes were never decorative — berzerk is a real command, and typing it still works. What's gone is the column that advertised them, which is the sort of thing a DOS user found out by typing dir anyway.

  • The game launchers are .exe files now. A launcher opens a program, so calling it berzerk.bat was describing it as a batch script, which it never was. The 351 named launchers — the ones carrying a link: — are now .EXE; the 408 numbered shortcuts and the menu.bat scripts genuinely are batch files and keep the extension. Nothing about running them changed: BERZERK, berzerk.exe and the menu number all still work, because the shell matches on the base name and checks for a link before it ever looks at the extension. TYPE BERZERK.EXE now does what DOS did — prints the program's bytes as CP437 glyphs, starting MZ, and stops dead at the first Ctrl-Z, which is why you get a short burst of noise rather than a screenful. The bytes are generated from the filename rather than stored, so each program has its own stable garbage and fs.js doesn't gain 351 blobs of it. Five byte values are shifted into printable range because the console acts on them instead of drawing them — bell, backspace, tab, linefeed, carriage return — exactly the five DOS's own console consumed. Deliberately absent: This program cannot be run in DOS mode. That string belongs to the Windows PE stub, and a machine pretending to be a DOS box has no business printing it.

Fixed

  • find has never once printed a game's title. It parses each menu's rows to learn what a launcher is called, and its pattern expected the echo and the box border to be adjacent — but the menus are padded out to centre the box, so the pattern matched no row anywhere on the site. Every result fell back to the launcher name printed twice: DEMON DEMON. The wiki's example output showed titles because it had been written from intent rather than from a run; it is now generated from the real thing. Fixing the pattern exposed the second half: with the code column gone there was nothing left to key titles on, so the index is rebuilt on the row number instead, which the numbered .bat beside each row resolves to a launcher (smb) or a sub-directory (cd bbc). That last part is new — directory rows never resolved before, so find acorn said ACORN ACORN and now says Acorn (1981).

  • Pokémon Red had a Greek capital theta in it. The font sheet is indexed by character code — charCodeAt picks the sprite directly, with no Unicode translation — so a glyph is written as whatever character sits at that code, which is why the box borders are spelled ÉÍ»º. The title had been written with a real é (U+00E9 = 233), and sprite 233 is CP437's Θ. It now holds char 130, CP437's actual é, written as \x82 rather than the invisible control character that lives at U+0082. Swept the other 397 menu titles for the same mistake: Odyssey² is the only other non-ASCII one and it was already right. Nothing else on the site renders a character it doesn't mean to. Searching for it was fixed properly rather than patched. find had a special case truncating any query starting with odyssey, because Odysseyý could never match what a person types; that is replaced by a fold applied to the title before matching — CP437's accented-letter block back to plain letters, 253 to 2 — plus a retry with spaces stripped from both sides. So find pokemon and find "odyssey 2" both land, and the next title that needs a trick glyph needs no code at all.

  • dir and find crashed on the three handhelds. The Game Boy, Lynx and Game Gear directories were written without the empty files and directories arrays every other directory has, and both commands index those arrays without checking. cd gameboy followed by dir threw, and find threw on any query, because it walks the whole tree and always reached them. Nothing about the games was wrong — six missing []s.

Added

  • The wiki has search. Forty-three pages had no way to find anything in them but the sidebar and guesswork. build.py now indexes every page by section — 505 of them — and generates a Search.html that filters as you type, ranks a heading match above a body match, and links each hit straight to the section anchor it came from. The interesting part is the content policy. These pages run default-src 'none' with no script-src, which is why not one of them has ever executed a line of JavaScript, and that is a property worth keeping. So the search page carries the standard policy plus the SHA-256 of exactly the script it contains — not 'unsafe-inline', which would permit any inline script on the page. The hash is computed as the script is written, in the same function, so the two cannot drift apart. Every other page in the wiki is untouched and still runs nothing. The index is inlined rather than fetched, so the page needs no connect-src and works from a downloaded copy with no server at all — fetch() of a local JSON would be blocked as cross-origin from file://. Section text is capped at 700 characters, which took the page from 224 KB gzipped to 107 KB while leaving every section findable by its heading and its opening.
